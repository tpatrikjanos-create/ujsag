# Product lens

Score: 6.5/10 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong

**One sentence pitch works.** "Create publication-quality wedding magazines and invitations in minutes without a designer." That's clear and compelling. The single-file web app with WYSIWYG live preview is elegant—drag, edit, see it happen.

**The first five minutes are sound.** Pick product type → choose a style pack → start editing → download or order. No account setup walls, no "10-step wizard hell." The contenteditable text and drag-and-drop layout are the right affordances for a non-designer. Real-time preview is non-negotiable for this product and you have it.

**Speed is a credible differentiator.** "In minutes" vs. "hire a designer for weeks and hundreds of euros" is a genuine experience advantage. The product delivers on this promise—the wizard gets you started fast and the editor is responsive.

**The monetization model is honest.** One-time purchase (no subscription dark patterns). Two paths (digital PDF vs. print) let the customer choose. No lock-in nonsense.

---

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)

**1. The print handoff is a seam where it breaks.** The customer sees a beautiful, vibrant design on screen in the WYSIWYG editor. They order 50 printed copies. Three weeks later, they open the box and the colors look flat, the text doesn't align exactly, the fold is off by 2mm, or the paper feels cheap. The entire value proposition is "professional quality without hiring a designer"—but if the printed artifact doesn't match the screen, the customer feels cheated. You've created an expectation you're not delivering. This is the single biggest risk. You don't yet know (or haven't told the board) whether print quality is consistently satisfying customers who ordered.

**2. No operational email system breaks the complete experience.** SPF/DKIM not set up means you can't reliably send order confirmations, proofs, tracking updates, or delivery notifications. A customer orders prints and hears nothing. Did it go through? Where is it? This isn't just a nice-to-have—it's a seam in the core purchase flow. The experience ends at "I clicked buy" and vanishes. For a product selling physical goods, this is a show-stopper.

**3. The handoff between design and physical fulfillment is opaque.** After ordering, what happens? Who prints it? What's the turnaround? Where does it go wrong? You're not owning the whole experience—you're handing the customer off to a printer (or printing partner) and hoping it works. There's no visibility into whether proofs are being reviewed, whether the customer can make changes, or what quality control actually happens. If you're not managing this end-to-end, you're not managing the product.

---

## The questions I would ask the founder

1. **On print quality:** You're live with users and in v6.47. What do you actually know about whether the printed magazines match customer expectations? Do you have data on complaints about color accuracy, paper quality, or layout precision? Have you ordered a sample yourself and compared it to the screen preview?

2. **On the complete purchase journey:** Walk me through what happens after a customer clicks "Order prints." What emails do they get? When? Can they review a proof before final production? How long until delivery? And—critically—if something goes wrong (colors are off, text is misaligned), what's the recovery? Can they reorder? Do you refund?

3. **On the one thing:** Is the product trying to do too much? The wizard has four style packs, two languages, admin mode to customize sections, both digital and print. What would happen if you deleted the weakest one? If you cut admin mode? If you nailed *only* the magazine with 2 style packs in Hungarian first?

4. **On decision-making at the start:** When a new customer lands and picks a style pack, how confident are they in their choice? Do style packs feel distinct enough? Or do they look similar and create paralysis? What happens to the customer who doesn't know if they want "Romantic" or "Modern"?

5. **On simplicity:** The editor with drag-and-drop, contenteditable text, and image upload—is it *truly* intuitive for a non-designer, or do customers get stuck? Where do they get stuck? Do you have drop-off data?

## If FUND IF: the conditions

1. **Nail the print quality seam.** Before scaling, you need proof that printed output matches screen expectations consistently. Order samples from your own printing partner. Compare them to the WYSIWYG preview side-by-side. If there's drift (colors, alignment, paper feel), fix it before more customers discover the gap.

2. **Complete the order-to-delivery experience.** Get email fully operational (SPF/DKIM configured). Design the email sequence: order confirmation, proof link (if applicable), production started, shipped, delivered. Make the customer feel like they're being taken care of, not abandoned after checkout.

3. **Cut ruthlessly to the core.** Pick ONE product type to perfect first (magazine seems strongest), pick TWO style packs max that feel genuinely different, and put "admin mode" and multi-language on a later roadmap. The product should feel focused, not like a toolbox with too many buttons.

4. **Measure what matters.** Before claiming product-market fit, you need clarity on: conversion rate from "start design" to "complete order," repeat rate (do couples reorder?), and customer satisfaction on the print quality itself. You're selling a physical product—satisfaction on the tangible artifact is your north star, not just web app usability.

5. **Own the handoff, not just the app.** If your printing partner is a black box, change it. You need visibility into turnaround, quality gates, and fulfillment. The product doesn't end when the customer downloads or clicks "buy"—it ends when they open the box.
